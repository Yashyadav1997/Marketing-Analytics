# Lesson 2. Validating a segmentation

A segmentation is a prediction in disguise. It claims that customers in a group will behave alike and differently from other groups. That claim can be tested the moment you have a period of behaviour the segmentation did not see, and it is striking how rarely it is.

The design is the one from Module 7: build every feature using only data up to the cutoff, freeze the scores, then compare them with what customers did afterward. Here the outcome is holdout revenue over 26 weeks. About 39% of customers ordered at all in the holdout, so the outcome is mostly zeros with a long right tail, a feature that will shape every metric below.

## Two metrics, two answers

`validate.py` scores six methods.

| Score | Rank correlation with holdout revenue | Share of holdout revenue captured by the top 10% |
|---|---|---|
| Recency only | 0.297 | 19.7% |
| Frequency only | 0.218 | 27.2% |
| Monetary only | 0.071 | 15.4% |
| RFM composite | 0.292 | 31.8% |
| BG/NBD value | 0.333 | 32.7% |
| Oracle (true parameters) | 0.714 | 45.8% |
| Random | 0 | 10% |

Look at recency. By rank correlation it looks nearly as good as the composite; by top-decile capture it is the weakest of the useful scores, at about twice what random selection would give. Why would the two metrics disagree?

Rank correlation is a statement about the entire ordering, and most of this population sits in a vast middle where the ordering decides whether a customer yields 0 or a modest amount. Recency is good at separating the active from the long-gone, which is most of the rank structure. But the top decile is about finding the *highest-value* customers, and recency alone cannot tell a frequent heavy buyer from an occasional one who happened to buy last week. Frequency does that, and so its top-decile capture is much better than its rank correlation would suggest.

Neither metric is the correct one. The correct one depends on the decision. If you are choosing the 10% to receive an expensive treatment, top-decile capture is the metric. If you are ordering a whole list for a gradually scaled campaign, rank correlation matters more. State the decision first, then choose the measure, then judge the segmentation.

## The ceiling

The oracle uses the true purchase rate, true spend and whether the customer is actually still active. It captures 45.8% of holdout revenue in its top decile, no more. That is the honest ceiling: even perfect knowledge of every customer's parameters lets you capture under half of holdout revenue with your top 10%, whereas the customers who in hindsight were the best 10% held 56%. The gap is chance, because the orders themselves are random draws. BG/NBD gets 32.7% against that ceiling, which is closer to perfect than the headline difference with RFM suggests. A reader who sees "33% versus 32%" and concludes "nothing to choose" has missed that the room for improvement was never 90 percentage points; it was about 13.

Real business data has no oracle, which is why the simulation is worth the trouble. It teaches what a good result looks like before you have any right to expect a better one.

## Is the difference real?

On the shipped seed, BG/NBD beats the RFM composite by under a point of top-decile capture. That might be noise. `robustness.py` regenerates the world ten times with different seeds and refits everything:

| Score | Top-10% capture, mean ± sd over 10 datasets |
|---|---|
| BG/NBD value | 33.4% ± 2.0 |
| RFM composite | 28.7% ± 2.1 |
| Frequency only | 27.5% ± 1.9 |
| Recency only | 20.7% ± 1.5 |

BG/NBD wins in all ten datasets, with an average margin of about five points (range 3.4 to 5.5; a wider check over twenty-two seeds gave the same range). The shipped dataset is the odd one out. There the gap is about one point, and a bootstrap over its customers puts a 95% interval of roughly -1.2 to +3.5 around it, which includes zero. Taken on its own, that sample would not let you say the model is better.

I do not have a tidy explanation for why this particular draw is so far from the others, and it would be wrong to invent one. It is left in deliberately. It shows what it feels like to hold a single sample that understates a method: the evidence in front of you says "no clear difference", and only the wider experiment says otherwise. In practice you rarely have parallel worlds, but you can split by time or by customer, bootstrap, and repeat, which gives some sense of the same variation. The honest report on the shipped data is "advantage not established here, typical advantage in comparable simulations about five points".

One caution on the scope of this result. These customers were generated by the model that BG/NBD assumes. On real data the margin could shrink, vanish or reverse, in which case RFM would be the sturdier choice precisely because it assumes less. Module 7's Exercise 4 showed how fast model-based forecasts degrade when behaviour shifts.

## Validating named segments

For rule-based segments the test is simpler: do the groups differ in outcome, by more than noise? Compare mean holdout revenue with a confidence interval for each:

| Segment | Mean holdout revenue | 95% interval |
|---|---|---|
| active repeat | 1,623 | 1,445 to 1,802 |
| cooling | 732 | 549 to 915 |
| lapsed | 364 | 233 to 494 |
| one-time | 548 | 487 to 610 |

Active repeat is clearly different from the rest. Cooling and lapsed are distinguishable, though not by a lot. One-time overlaps neither cleanly, a symptom of the tenure problem described in Lesson 1. If two segments' intervals overlap heavily, you are paying to maintain a distinction the data cannot support.
