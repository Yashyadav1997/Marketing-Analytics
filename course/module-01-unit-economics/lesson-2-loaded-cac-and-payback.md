# Lesson 2. Loaded CAC and payback

## The number the platform gives you

An ad platform reports cost per acquisition as spend divided by the conversions it attributes to itself. That is a statement about media efficiency. Customer acquisition cost, in the sense a finance team would use it, is the whole cost of getting a customer: media, but also the agency retainer, the people who make the creative, the tools that run the funnel, and some share of the in-house team's time.

```
loaded CAC = (ad spend + agency fees + creative production + tools + attributed team time)
             ÷ new customers acquired
```

Take a month with Rs 5,00,000 of media, Rs 1,50,000 of agency fees, Rs 1,20,000 of creative production, Rs 30,000 of tooling and Rs 50,000 of team time, which brought in 1,000 genuinely new customers. Loaded CAC is Rs 850. If the platform's own figure was Rs 500, the loaded cost is 70% higher.

That 70% is in line with a rule of thumb you will meet in practitioner writing, that loaded CAC tends to run 60 to 90% above platform CAC. Hold it loosely. It comes from one consultant's portfolio and depends heavily on how much overhead a brand carries relative to media. A brand spending Rs 50 lakh a month on ads and Rs 2 lakh on everything else will show a much smaller load. The useful thing to take away is the structure of the calculation, because the structure tells you which of your own costs to go and find.

## Three decisions that change the answer

**The denominator.** "New customers" and "orders" are not the same thing. Platforms count conversions, and a repeat buyer who clicks an ad is a conversion without being an acquisition. If a fifth of your attributed conversions are returning customers, platform CAC is flattered and loaded CAC computed on orders is too. Use first-time buyers from your own order system.

**The cost boundary.** Where do you stop? Including a share of the founder's time is defensible and almost never done. Excluding brand-building spend makes performance channels look cheaper than the whole system really is. There is no single correct boundary; there is only the discipline of writing yours down and holding it constant, so that the trend means something even if the level is arguable.

**The period.** Costs and customers do not land in the same month. Creative produced in March may drive customers in April and May. For a stable brand it washes out; for a brand mid-launch it can swing CAC by a third. Use a trailing quarter when in doubt.

## Payback

Loaded CAC tells you what a customer costs. Payback asks how long the business waits to be repaid, which is a cash question as much as a profitability one. The textbook form is

```
payback (months) = loaded CAC ÷ (AOV × margin × orders per month)
```

With CAC of 850, an average order value of 1,200, a margin of 58% and 0.6 orders a month, gross profit per customer-month is 417.6 and payback is about 2.0 months.

Two things about that example before you admire it. First, 0.6 orders a month, more than seven a year, is a very high purchase frequency; it suits a consumable bought constantly, not most beauty or apparel brands. Second, and more fundamentally, the formula assumes a customer buys at a steady rate starting immediately. Real repeat behaviour looks nothing like that. Many customers buy once. Of those who return, the rate typically falls with each month since acquisition.

That is why the exercise in this module has you compute payback a second way, from observed cohorts: add up the cumulative gross profit per customer by month since acquisition and find the month it crosses CAC. With the synthetic data shipped here, the constant-rate formula says the paid-social channel pays back in 2.4 months. The cohort data says 3.7. The formula was optimistic by more than a month, and in a business running on a thin cash cushion, a month is not trivial.

The error does not always run the same way. For the brand-search channel the formula says 1.1 months while cohorts show the customer is profitable on the first order. A first-order-profitable channel is repaid immediately; the formula, by spreading profit over time, overstated the wait. The lesson is not that the formula is biased in one direction. It is that it is an assumption about the shape of repeat behaviour, and you can observe that shape.

## What to do with it

State payback by channel and by acquisition cohort, with the cohort curve beside it. Where a channel's payback exceeds your cash runway, say so plainly, because that is the point at which a good-looking campaign becomes a financing problem.
