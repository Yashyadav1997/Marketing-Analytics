# Case study: Lumen Skin and the Diwali budget

*Lumen Skin is fictional. The setting borrows its structure from the kind of category, Indian direct-to-consumer skincare, that practitioners track closely, but every figure below is invented for teaching.*

## Situation

Lumen Skin sells a serum range through its own site and, increasingly, through marketplaces. It is eighteen months old, profitable at the gross level, and cash-tight because it pre-buys inventory ahead of the festive season. The founder has been told by the agency that paid social is "working at 3.4x ROAS" and wants to raise the October media budget from Rs 20 lakh to Rs 35 lakh.

You have been asked for a view before Friday.

## What you are given

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Average order value | Rs 1,150 |
| Gross margin after shipping and payment fees | 52% |
| Return and refund rate (non-resalable) | 9% |
| Platform-reported CAC (paid social) | Rs 520 |
| Monthly agency fee | Rs 1.8 lakh |
| Creative production (monthly) | Rs 1.4 lakh |
| Tools and analytics | Rs 0.4 lakh |
| In-house marketing team time attributed to acquisition | Rs 0.9 lakh |
| Share of platform-attributed conversions that are repeat customers (from CRM match) | about 22% |
| Month-3 repeat rate, paid-social cohorts | 14% |
| Month-3 repeat rate, organic and brand-search cohorts | 23% |
| Cash runway at current burn, before festive inventory | about 4.5 months |

Media in the last quarter averaged Rs 20 lakh a month, and the platform attributed about 3,850 conversions a month.

## Questions

1. What is the floor ROAS on first-order economics, once returns are included? Is 3.4x comfortably above it?
2. Rebuild CAC. Remember the denominator problem: not all attributed conversions are new customers. What do you get?
3. Using the repeat rates provided and any further assumptions you state, estimate payback for paid-social customers. How does the answer compare with the founder's runway?
4. What happens to CAC when budget rises by 75%? You have no data on that response. What do you recommend doing about the fact that you do not?
5. Write the memo.

## Instructor notes

Some of the interest is in the arithmetic and some in what the arithmetic leaves open.

**Floor.** Per 100 orders, reported revenue is Rs 1,15,000 and COGS (including shipping and payment fees) is 48% of it, Rs 55,200. Nine returned orders are refunded and the goods are lost, so retained revenue is Rs 1,04,650 and profit Rs 49,450. That is 43 paise per reported rupee, so the floor against *reported* ROAS is about 2.33x (against retained revenue, margin is 47.3% and the floor 2.12x). 3.4x clears it, but the headroom is 3.4 × 0.43 − 1, about Rs 0.46 of profit per rupee of media, rather than anything the headline suggests, and that is before payroll and overhead.

**CAC.** Roughly 22% of the 3,850 conversions are repeat buyers, leaving about 3,000 true acquisitions. Loaded costs are Rs 20 lakh media plus 1.8 + 1.4 + 0.4 + 0.9 = Rs 4.5 lakh of other costs, Rs 24.5 lakh, over about 3,000 new customers: roughly Rs 815. Against the platform's Rs 520, the load is about 57%. Decent students will notice that most of the gap comes from the denominator, not the overhead.

**Payback.** First-order gross profit per order placed is 0.91 × 1,150 − 0.48 × 1,150, about Rs 495, so a first order covers roughly three fifths of the Rs 815 loaded CAC. The 14% month-3 repeat rate for paid social means recovery of the remainder will take many months at that repeat rate, comfortably longer than the runway allows once festive inventory is bought. A strong answer separates "the economics are sound over a long horizon" from "the cash plan cannot finance them".

**Scale.** Raising spend 75% almost always raises marginal CAC because the best audiences are used first. A strong memo proposes a staged increase with a pre-registered stopping rule, and, if feasible, a holdout so that the effect on *total* sales, not on platform-reported conversions, can be seen.

**What a good memo avoids.** Anchoring on 3.4x; presenting a single payback number without a range; implying the repeat rates will hold at higher spend; ignoring the cash constraint because the long-run LTV looks acceptable.
