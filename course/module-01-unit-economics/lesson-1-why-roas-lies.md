# Lesson 1. Why ROAS lies

Return on ad spend is revenue divided by advertising cost. It is easy to compute, instantly available, and silent about the one thing a business needs to know: how much of that revenue was left over after paying for the goods.

Consider a brand selling a Rs 1,000 product that costs Rs 420 to make, pack, ship and collect payment for. Its gross margin is 58%. If a campaign returns 3x, each rupee of spend brings back Rs 3 of revenue and Rs 1.74 of gross profit. The campaign is comfortably ahead. Now move the same 3x onto a product with a 40% margin: Rs 1.20 of gross profit per rupee spent. The campaign is still ahead, but by twenty paise, which is before anyone has paid for the warehouse, the team, the returns or the tools. Same ROAS, quite different business.

## The floor

Setting gross profit equal to ad spend gives the break-even ROAS:

```
break-even ROAS = 1 / gross margin
```

At 58% that is 1.72x; at 40% it is 2.5x. Below that line a first order loses money outright. This is a floor, not a target. A defensible target adds the costs that sit between gross margin and profit, which is why the next refinement is to replace gross margin with *contribution margin* (gross margin less the other variable costs, such as fulfilment, returns and discounts that your COGS line missed).

> A useful habit: whenever someone quotes a ROAS, ask for the margin it was earned on. If nobody knows, the number has not yet been interpreted.

## Where the floor itself goes wrong

Three complications deserve more attention than the formula does.

**Returns and discounts.** If 12% of orders come back, the revenue in the numerator is overstated and the floor is higher than it appears. Many dashboards report gross merchandise value at the point of sale.

**First order versus lifetime.** Break-even ROAS on a first order is deliberately harsh. A brand with strong repeat purchase can rationally run below it, accepting a first-order loss to be repaid by later orders. That is a bet on retention, and Lessons 2 and 3 show how to price it. What the floor gives you is a clear statement of the bet: "we are paying X today and expecting to recover it by month Y."

**Whose revenue?** Platform ROAS counts conversions the platform claims. If the customer would have bought anyway (through brand search, or after an email), the platform may be claiming revenue it did not cause. The floor is only as honest as the revenue figure above it. Module 5 deals with this; for now just notice that every number in this module is attributed revenue and inherits that weakness.

## What to do with it

Compute break-even ROAS for each product line, not for the business. A catalogue with 70% margin items and 35% margin items has two floors. Campaigns that quietly sell the low-margin product at the high-margin floor are the ones that report success while the bank balance disagrees.
